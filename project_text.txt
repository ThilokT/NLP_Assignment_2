Project 1: N-gram Language Modeling, Smoothing, and Neural LM
Reproduction
Problem Statement
A language model assigns a probability to a word sequencew 1, . . . , wn: the higher the probability, the more fluent
the model considers that sequence. A classical n-gram model does this purely by counting – e.g. a trigram model
estimatesP(w i |w i−2, wi−1)≈count(w i−2, wi−1, wi)/count(w i−2, wi−1) directly from a training corpus. This
is simple and fully transparent, but it fails in a specific, severe way: if the exact trigram “the arctic fox” never
once occurred in training, the model assigns it probabilityexactly zero– even though it is a completely ordinary
English phrase. Because sentence probability is a product of such terms, one zero-probability trigram drags the
entiresentence’s probability to zero, no matter how fluent the rest of it is. This is thedata-sparsityproblem, and
it worsens asngrows.Smoothingis the classical fix: instead of assigning zero probability to anything unseen, it
reserves a small amount of probability mass for sequences the model never observed. Task 1 asks you to implement
three such smoothing methods and measure directly how much each one helps.
A neural language model sidesteps this failure differently: rather than requiring an exact count-table lookup, it
represents each word as a continuous vector and predicts the next word from that representation, so an unseen phrase
can still receive a reasonable probability as long as its individual words and the surrounding pattern resemble things
the model has seen. Task 2 reproduces a well-known such model; Task 3 then asks the project’s central question – if
a neural model already handles unseen sequences gracefully on its own, does classical smoothing still add anything
once combined with it? Every model is evaluated with perplexity, PPL = exp

Project 2: Statistical vs. Neural Sequence Labeling
Problem Statement
Taggingmeans assigning one category label to every word in a sentence – e.g. part-of-speech (POS) tagging labels
“the dog runs” as (DET, NOUN, VERB); named-entity recognition (NER) instead tags each word as part of a
person/organization/location name, or not. This project builds two very different taggers, then combines them, on
whichever tagging problem your assigned dataset defines.
AHidden Markov Model (HMM)tagger searches for the tag sequence maximizingP(tags)×P(words|tags), using
transition probabilitiesP(tag i |tag i−1) andemission probabilitiesP(word i |tag i) estimated by counting, decoded
efficiently withViterbi decoding. Just like the n-gram counts in Project 1, this table faces the same sparsity problem,
which is why Task 1 smooths it. A neural tagger instead learns dense word representations directly from data; Task
2 reproduces aBiLSTM(reads the sentence both directions) that outputs anemission scoreper tag per word –
the learned counterpart of the HMM’s emission table – topped with aCRFlayer that, like the HMM’s transition
table, scores tag-to-tag likelihood so the best whole sequence is chosen, not each word independently. Task 3 asks
whether keeping the HMM’s simple, inspectable Viterbi decoding but replacing its hand-counted emission table
with the BiLSTM’s learned scores produces a hybrid that stays transparent while closing (some of) the accuracy
gap.

Project 3: From CKY to Neural Grammar Induction
Problem Statement
A constituency tree captures which words group together into phrases in a sentence – e.g. “the dog runs” brackets
as [S [NP the dog] [VP runs]]. A Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar (PCFG) is a set of rewrite rules (e.g. S→
NP VP), each with a probability; a full tree’s probability is the product of every rule used to build it. Given a
PCFG, the CKY algorithm finds the single highest-probability parse tree for a sentence efficiently, bottom-up. A
PCFG’s rule probabilities are normally estimated by counting from a treebank of gold, human-annotated trees –
so the grammar is only ever as good as the (expensive, limited) supply of gold trees available. This project asks
whether a grammar can instead be learned directly from raw, unlabeled sentences with no trees provided anywhere
–grammar induction– and how the result compares to the classical, fully-supervised approach.
Task 1 builds the classical pipeline: estimate PCFG rule probabilities from your assigned partition’s gold training
trees, then parse with CKY. Task 2 reproduces an unsupervised approach: rather than counted rule probabilities,
it introduces a continuous latent variable per sentence and predicts each rule’s probability from that variable with
a neural network, training the whole grammar to explain your assigned partition’s trainingsentences alone– no
gold tree is ever shown to it, even though (unlike a typical unsupervised-induction setup) this training corpus is
necessarily small, since it is the same few thousand sentences as your partition rather than a separate, much larger
raw-text corpus; this is an intentional trade-off of the one-dataset-per-group design, and you should expect Task
2’s induced grammar to be weaker than published compound-PCFG results that train on far more raw text. Task 3
asks the natural next question: keep Task 2’s neural rule-parameterization, but train it directly on your partition’s
gold trees instead of inducing it unsupervised – does a neurally-parameterized but fully-supervised grammar beat
the classical, purely count-based one?

Project 5: From Static to Contextual – Distributional Semantics and
What’s Inside BERT
Problem Statement
This project studies how word representations move from classicalstaticvectors to moderncontextualrepresenta-
tions. You will first build classical PPMI+SVD word vectors and evaluate them on semantic benchmarks (Task 1).
You will then reproduce a structural probe on frozen BERT representations to determine which BERT layer most
strongly encodes syntactic structure (Task 2). Finally, you will use that layer to build BERT-derived static vectors
by pooling contextual representations and compare them with the classical vectors on both semantic and syntactic
evaluations (Task 3).
Why this is interesting.The classical approach is based on thedistributional hypothesis: a word’s meaning
can be inferred from the contexts in which it occurs. This produces one fixed vector per word. BERT instead
produces a different representation for a word depending on its context. Task 2 asks whether syntactic information
is recoverable from these contextual representations and which layer encodes it most strongly. Task 3 then asks
whether useful information from these contextual representations survives when they are pooled into a single static
vector.
What you will compare.The project evaluates two complementary properties of the representations. WordSim-
353 and Google analogies measuresemantic/lexical structure, while the dependency-tree experiments measure
syntactic structure. The two evaluations may not agree: a representation can improve semantic performance while
losing some syntactic information, or vice versa. Understanding this difference is part of the goal of the project.

A word is usedmetaphoricallywhen its contextual meaning departs from its most basic, literal sense ,e.g. in “the
companydigestedits rival,”digestedis not literally about a stomach. Metaphor is pervasive in ordinary language
(estimates put roughly one in every seven or eight content words in running English text as metaphorical), yet it is
invisible to most NLP pipelines: a parser or SRL system treats “digested” identically whether the sentence is about
a stomach or a merger. Metaphordetection, flagging which words in a sentence are used metaphorically which is
a prerequisite for any downstream system that needs to distinguish literal from figurative meaning. The classical
approach formalizes a specific linguistic theory of what makes a word metaphorical: theSelectional Preference
Violation(SPV) hypothesis holds that a word is metaphorical when it violates the typical semantic category of
arguments its literal sense prefers , verbs like “digest” literally prefer a food-type object, so pairing it with an
abstract object like “a rival” signals metaphor. This can be operationalized purely from corpus co-occurrence
statistics, with no learned parameters. A neural approach instead treats metaphor detection as token classification:
label every word in a sentence as literal or metaphorical directly from contextual embeddings, letting the model
discover whatever signal separates the two classes on its own, whether or not it resembles selectional violation.
Task 3 asks the project’s central question – does the neural model’s decision boundary actually track selectional-
preference violation the way the classical theory predicts, or does it succeed (or fail) for entirely different reasons?

T ask 1 — Baseline: Selectional-Preference Violation Detector (30 marks)
Objective.Build a fully classical, non-neural metaphor detector on your assigned dataset using selectional-preference
statistics only.
Step-by-step instructions:
1. Preprocess: tokenize, POS-tag, and dependency-parse your assigned dataset’s train/val/test splits.
2. Build selectional-preference profiles: for every predicate governing a subject/object relation in your training split,
collect the WordNet supersense of each filler and estimateP(c|v) per predicate.
3. Compute Resnik’s Selectional Preference Strength and per-token association score log P(c actual|v)P(c actual) for every contentword.
4. Threshold the association score (grid search on validation, maximize F1) to output literal/metaphorical.
5. Fall back to majority-class (literal) for tokens with no governing predicate or zero co-occurrence count.
6. Evaluate on your assigned dataset’s test split.
Evaluation metrics to report:
•Precision, recall, F1 (metaphor class) on your assigned dataset.
•F1 by POS (verb/noun/adj/prep).
